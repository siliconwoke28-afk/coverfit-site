Book trim sizes

Trim size is the finished page size after cutting. It changes the feel of the book, the spine width, the cover file, and sometimes the printing cost. Pick it before you design anything.

The most common mistake is choosing a size that looks good on screen but is unusual for the genre.

What the size signals

Readers do not consciously measure a book, but they read its proportions. A 5 × 8 paperback feels like a mass-market novel; a 5.5 × 8.5 feels like a literary trade paperback; a 6 × 9 reads as business or serious nonfiction; an 8.5 × 11 reads as a workbook or manual.

Matching the genre convention is not a rule, but it reduces friction. A 6 × 9 romance looks slightly off on a shelf of 5.5 × 8.5 romances, and readers notice before they can explain why.

How trim affects everything downstream

  • Spine width depends on page count and paper, not trim, but the full wrap width includes twice the trim.
  • Cover dimensions are computed from trim plus spine plus bleed. A larger trim means a larger cover file and more pixels at 300 DPI.
  • Printing cost is driven mainly by page count and ink, with some trim-based minimums. Larger pages can fit more words per page, which reduces page count.
  • Interior margins need to grow with page count; a 500-page book needs a deeper gutter than a 100-page book.

Choosing between 5.5 × 8.5 and 6 × 9

If the book is narrative, choose 5.5 × 8.5. It feels more intimate and is the default for trade fiction and memoir. If the book is prescriptive, has charts, or is sold to a professional audience, choose 6 × 9. It reads as a reference and gives more room for tables.

Do not choose a size because a template was available. Choose the size, then generate the cover with the matching trim. The cover maker recalculates the wrap and spine when you change trim.
