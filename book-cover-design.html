Design guide

Book cover design that survives the thumbnail

A cover has two jobs: be legible at 200 pixels wide in a search result, and be printable at 300 DPI. Those two constraints decide almost everything else.

Judge every draft at thumbnail size first. If the title is not readable there, the detail work does not matter.

Start with the thumbnail test

On a category page, your cover is roughly 200 pixels tall. At that size, a title set in a thin serif at 30 points disappears, a busy photograph turns to mud, and a clever visual pun becomes invisible. Shrink your draft to 200 pixels wide and look at it on a phone. If you cannot read the title and guess the genre in two seconds, redesign before adding anything.

This is why so many bestsellers look blunt: huge type, one image, one color field. Blunt survives compression.

Build a hierarchy, not a collage

A cover needs three levels of information, in this order:

  1. Title. The largest element, usually 30 to 50 percent of the cover height. It can be one word or three; it should never need a caption.
  2. Author. Second largest, usually at the bottom or directly under the title. For a debut, smaller than you want; for a known author, it can sit at the top.
  3. Everything else. Subtitle, series line, review quote, badge. If a cover needs more than one of these, choose one and cut the rest.

Every additional element competes with the title. The most common self-published cover problem is not a bad image; it is five good elements fighting for the same space.

Type choices that behave in print

Two families are enough: a display face for the title and a text face for everything else. Avoid very light weights, hairline serifs, and scripts below 24 points; they fill in or break up at print resolution.

  • Thrillers and crime: condensed sans, heavy weights, high contrast against a dark field.
  • Literary fiction: serif or high-contrast display, generous spacing, restrained palette.
  • Romance: script for one word at most, paired with a clean sans; never a full title in script at thumbnail size.
  • Business and nonfiction: bold sans, two colors, one geometric shape. The title should read like a statement.
  • Children's books: rounded, warm, high-saturation; the title can be playful, but contrast still wins.

Color is a genre signal before it is a taste choice

Readers use color to sort categories before they read a word. Dark blue and black signal thriller; pastel and cream signal romance or women's fiction; orange and yellow signal business; saturated primaries signal children's. Ignoring the convention is allowed, but it makes the thumbnail do less work.

Pick one dominant color and one accent. A cover with five colors reads as noise at small size. Test in grayscale: if the hierarchy collapses, the design depends on color alone and will fail for color-blind readers and in black-and-white thumbnails.

The spine is part of the design

On a shelf, the spine is the only visible surface. Keep the title and author legible, use the same background color as the front, and do not put critical art there. Below 0.25 in, skip text entirely. Use the spine width calculator before you lay it out; the number changes with page count and paper stock.

The back cover has a job

The back cover is not a poster. It has to do three things: state what the book is, give one reason to read it, and leave room for the barcode. A workable structure is a short headline, two or three sentences of blurb, one line of author bio, and a quiet lower-right corner.

Keep the barcode area free. KDP prints a barcode roughly 2 × 1.2 in in the lower-right; art or type underneath it will be covered.

Common mistakes that cost sales

  • Designing at screen resolution. A 6 × 9 cover at 72 DPI is 432 × 648 px; print needs about 1,800 × 2,700 px before bleed.
  • Forgetting the bleed. Background art must extend 0.125 in past the trim or you get a white sliver after cutting.
  • Using a font you cannot license. KDP requires rights to every asset. System fonts are safe; random downloads often are not.
  • Reusing a cover across page counts. The spine moves, and the front cover shifts with it.
  • Judging on a large monitor. The buyer sees a thumbnail, not your 27-inch preview.
